# TutorSkillGraph research proposal

[Project overview and dataset notes](README.md) · [中文](RESEARCH_BRIEF.zh-CN.md)

**Can an AI tutor use the reasons students give on a later, independent task to discover what an earlier lesson left unresolved and improve the next lesson?** The tutor would learn when to use a particular example, which hints to give and which piece of reasoning to leave to the learner. This proposal describes the design and its first testable deliverable. The classroom exchanges and revisions below are illustrations. Learning gains are an empirical question.

## Why a correct answer can lead a tutor astray

A student gets a question right and the lesson moves on. Sometimes the question admits the right answer both from the intended principle and from a shortcut that happens to work. If the learner leaves with the shortcut, the tutor can file the lesson as a success and teach it again. The difference surfaces when the learner meets a new problem alone.

In a high-school field experiment, access to a general GPT interface improved practice performance, yet that group scored below the control group on the subsequent unassisted exam. A physics instruction study brings the issue closer to lesson design. With the same contrasting cases, one group received the formula before practice and another tried to invent the relationship before hearing the explanation. They performed similarly when applying formulas to word problems, but the latter group transferred the ratio structure more often to other topics. These results give us a reason to examine the materials, their order and the work the learner gets to do. [High-school mathematics experiment](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) · [Contrasting cases and transfer](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/assets/papers/2011/Practicing_versus_inventing.pdf)

The proposed mechanism is specific. **When an independent response reveals a possible misconception, replay that explanation against the original lesson. Could it also have produced the earlier correct answer?** If so, look for an activity on which the intended principle and the competing explanation predict different answers. The earlier lesson may have allowed the misconception to persist or simply failed to reveal it. A subsequent comparison can test whether the edit helps.

Results depend on the instructional conditions. In a study of 181 students, delaying instruction or providing contrasting cases did not produce the anticipated gains in conceptual understanding. This makes it worth checking whether the chosen materials distinguish the explanation a particular learner actually gave. [Study of contrasts and instructional order](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-020-09504-7)

## A density lesson from first decision to revision

The American Chemical Society's middle-school unit uses equal-volume cubes with different masses and rods with identical masses of 15 grams but different volumes. Students can predict, measure and compare. The materials are real. Suppose a learner using them says, “You can tell how dense something is just by weighing it.” [Density unit](https://www.acs.org/middleschoolchemistry/lessonplans/chapter3.html) · [Equal-mass rod activity](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-3/lesson-2/ch3-l2-lesson-plan.pdf)

1. **Teach the original lesson.** Ask the student to predict the densities of two 15-gram rods, explain the prediction and then measure. Mass alone predicts a tie. The rod with less volume has the greater density. The activity challenges the claim the student actually made.
2. **Identify what the answer leaves open.** After measuring, the student says the smaller rod is denser. That fits both “at equal mass, less volume means greater density” and “smaller things are always denser.” Record whether the student introduced the equal-mass condition or the tutor had already explained it.
3. **Read the independent response.** Ask what happens when a uniform lump of clay is cut into unequal pieces. “The small one is denser because smaller things are always denser” identifies a specific candidate explanation. A wrong option alone gives weaker evidence. “They are equally dense because mass and volume decrease in proportion” supports a different interpretation.
4. **Revise the next lesson.** If the size-only explanation recurs for learners with similar starting points, keep the rod comparison and add the cut-clay contrast. Ask the student to explain why the smaller item was denser with the rods but equally dense with the clay. When the student has given only an answer, first elicit a reason. A student who needs the meanings of mass and volume can receive a direct explanation.
5. **Test the revision.** Once the clay problem is taught, it becomes practice. Test the next cohort independently on fresh materials or measurements, for instance two unfamiliar samples differing in both mass and volume, and ask for the reason. Match lesson time, example count and assessment difficulty across designs.

## What changes in the graph

**The unit of revision is an instructional hypothesis.** For a learner who has expressed a particular idea, what relationship might they discover by working with this material, with this much guidance, at this point in the lesson? Its components recur across activities. Their roles within this specific activity matter.

| Record | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Conditions visible before teaching | The student's stated reasoning and work, clues already provided, the task goal and available materials. Store the utterance separately from the tutor's interpretation. |
| Nodes and hyperedges | Nodes can represent expressed judgments, material constraints and teaching skills such as eliciting a prediction, comparing cases and asking for an explanation. One role-labelled hyperedge binds them into a design. It records order, the quantity held fixed and the idea under examination. |
| What actually happened | Link the materials shown, clues disclosed and the learner's subsequent contribution to a turn or classroom event. Preserve both the planned activity and its observed enactment. |
| Evidence after teaching | Within each study, link the same learner's independent response, item and reason where available to the design version that was used. The tutor reads this evidence when revising later lessons. |

A complete written skill can describe the density lesson. A possible advantage of the graph appears when materials and moves recur across many lessons. Both equal-mass rods and pieces of the same substance can be used with “predict, then compare,” but they discriminate different explanations. Retrieval starts from the student's expressed judgment and checks what each candidate explanation predicts for the available materials. A revision might add one contrast to a design or narrow its conditions of use. Another lesson could reuse the rationale for selecting that contrast. A written skill or condition table can implement the same ideas. The comparison is whether the representation improves retrieval or revision with the same information.

## How a tutor would revise a design

1. **Recover a verifiable episode.** Use a model API to locate relevant utterances or timestamps in anonymized records. Link the goal, learner's explanation, tutor's hints, response and item. A learner who first names a relationship after the teacher points to it may leave the same text answer as a learner who discovered it independently.
2. **Find the ambiguity.** Derive candidate explanations from the student's words. Write down each prediction for the old and later tasks, and check those predictions against the actual task and answer. Use scores as outcome evidence. Where reasoning is ambiguous, inspect an available explanation or propose a task on which the two accounts disagree.
3. **Make a targeted edit.** Compare episodes with similar prior performance, difficulty and help already received, including examples that contradict the apparent pattern. Change a material, the timing of a hint or the request for an explanation. Keep the old and new designs, the supporting and contrary cases and a situation in which the versions lead to different teaching decisions.
4. **Decide whether to adopt the edit.** Historical episodes can motivate a change and show how it affects future choices. To estimate its effect on learning, compare learners who actually receive the two versions. A calculation slip, misreading or lower prior knowledge may also explain an error. Record these alternatives alongside the proposed interpretation.

## What would count as progress

**Can we observe the proposed difference?** Start with sessions linked to an aligned transfer item. Audit extracted turns against the originals and report how often reasons, steps or only final answers are visible. Hold out students. With comparable starting points, task information and amount of help, test whether adding the instructional combination and actual reasoning improves prediction of independent performance and specific errors. Reserve later responses for retrospective evaluation and training-set revisions.

**Does a hypergraph help locate the edit?** Give the same model three editable representations: complete written skills, a structured condition table and a role- and order-aware hypergraph. Match cases, source passages, outcome feedback, candidate materials and model-call budgets. On new combinations, check whether designs distinguish the rival explanations, target the observed misconception and predict a difference between the old and new lessons. Educators blinded to the system can check the student quotes and tasks. Verify calculable predictions directly. Cross the representation comparison with the feedback source, using independent work or just turn scores. If written rules work as well, use the simpler implementation.

**Do students learn more?** In a prospective comparison, randomly assign comparable learners to the original or revised instruction. Measure independent accuracy and reasoning on untaught items. Match time and amount of practice so that a diagnostic contrast has a fair comparison with ordinary extra practice. Report historical prediction, quality of a proposed edit and observed student learning separately.

**First deliverable.** Use a model API and an ordinary CPU to produce a small set of auditable cases. For each, save the source episode, information known at decision time, the two explanations and their predictions, an aligned independent outcome, a proposed edit and a result that would count against it. Complete one grounded equation-writing case, then examine held-out learners and tasks. Dataset entry points and handoff notes are in the [project overview](README.md).
